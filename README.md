# The Vault of the Starving Mind

A lethal D&D one-shot grinder built around survival, exploration, greed, and player choice.

## Overview

**The Vault of the Starving Mind** is a large-scale dungeon escape event for **8–12 players**, with all characters starting at **level 3** and capped at **40 hit points maximum**.

The dungeon is run by **Xhal’theris, the Velvet Maw**, a Mind Flayer who has taken the characters prisoner and placed them inside his private death game. The characters are not here to save the world. They are here to survive, escape, and decide how much risk they are willing to take for real-world prizes.

The dungeon is built as a massive physical layout using **Dwarven Forge terrain**, stretching roughly **5' x 7'** across the play area. It contains dozens of rooms, corridors, traps, puzzles, hazards, monsters, hidden areas, prize rooms, shortcuts, locked routes, and escape paths.

What the players see on the table is the dungeon. The Players will start at one end of the Table locked in different rooms within the caves. They'll be able to join forces pretty quickly. The dungeon is H shaped with with the players needing to find where the exit is located. The middle (lava/ruins) seperated Caves from Stone Dungeon. 

At the End of the Dungeon is one final puzzle that require all 7 Crystal Shards placed in the right order.  


## Core Premise

The characters wake up inside the dungeon as prisoners of Xhal’theris. Though they don't know it yet. They have no idea of how they got there or why.  The dungeon is not designed as a heroic quest. It is a survival game.

The players must decide whether to:

- push toward the exit
- search for better weapons and supplies
- explore dangerous side rooms
- hunt for prize crystals
- take shortcuts
- solve puzzles
- avoid monsters
- risk their lives for better rewards

Death is final. If a character dies, that player is out and their treasure stays on their body.. 

## Player Starting Conditions

Characters begin at **level 3**.

They start with:

- basic light armor
- basic weapons
- very limited spell materials
- little or no useful adventuring gear
- no meaningful surplus supplies

Anything better must be found inside the dungeon.

This includes:

- better weapons
- armor upgrades
- shields
- ammunition
- rope
- tools
- spell components
- arcane focuses
- holy symbols
- healing supplies
- keys
- maps
- clues
- magic items

The dungeon should reward scavenging, planning, and opportunism.

## Death and Elimination

This is a grinder.

When a character dies:

- the player is out
- there are no replacement characters
- the dead character’s gear and crystals remain in the dungeon
- surviving characters may loot the body if they can reach it

This should be explained clearly before play begins.

The point is not balanced heroic survival. The point is pressure, risk, and consequence.

## Rest Rules

Short rests are limited and costly.

- A short rest takes **15 minutes of real-world time**
- The party has a maximum of **three short rests total**
- Each short rest allows a character to spend only **one Hit Die**
- Long rests are not available

Resting should feel useful but dangerous. Safe areas should be rare, and some apparent safe rooms may be traps.

## Prize Crystal System

Hidden throughout the dungeon are seven physical prize crystals.

The exit seal can only be opened by collecting all seven crystal colors and placing them into the exit lock.

If a character dies, any crystals they carry remain in the dungeon.

Crystals may be traded, stolen, dropped, hidden, or looted from the dead.

### Crystal Tiers (Read with The Scrying Stone)

Each crystal color is tied to one prize tier and one clue phrase that can only be read clearly through the physical prop, **The Scrying Stone**. Each clue points toward the location of another crystal.

## Dungeon Design Philosophy

The dungeon should not be a linear path.

It should be a dangerous, interconnected environment with:

- multiple starting locations
- optional rooms
- side routes
- locked routes
- gear-gated paths
- shortcuts
- dead ends
- hidden rewards
- roaming enemies
- puzzle rooms
- traps
- visible temptations
- escalating danger

The safest path should not be the most rewarding path.

The most rewarding path should not be the safest path.

## Core Gameplay Loop

The core loop is simple:

1. Survive the current room.
2. Search for resources.
3. Decide whether to push forward or explore.
4. Take risks for better gear or prize crystals.
5. Avoid wasting time, noise, and hit points.
6. Escape alive if possible.

The players should always be choosing between:

- safety and reward
- speed and exploration
- teamwork and self-interest
- greed and survival

## Xhal’theris, the Velvet Maw

**Xhal’theris** is the Mind Flayer host of the dungeon.

He acts as:

- jailer
- announcer
- game master
- observer
- tormentor
- prize master
- rule-enforcer

He does not need to be physically present in every room. He can speak through psychic projection, carved mouths, crystal eyes, statues, walls, or dungeon mechanisms.

His purpose is to reinforce the tone of the event.

He may announce:

- deaths
- unlocked rooms
- opened routes
- approaching monsters
- prize reveals
- changes in dungeon state
- countdowns
- taunts
- offers
- bargains

Xhal’theris should be cruel, amused, theatrical, and clinical. He views the players as contestants and specimens.

## Suggested Dungeon Zones

The exact layout can vary, but the dungeon works best when divided into distinct zones.

### 1. Cell Blocks

Starting area for several player pairs.

Purpose:

- establish the premise
- provide basic survival choices
- introduce weak enemies
- hide first tools and clues
- allow some groups to begin moving toward each other

### 2. Service Tunnels

A connecting network of narrow routes, crawlspaces, shafts, and hidden passages.

Purpose:

- allow movement between sectors
- create ambush opportunities
- reward rope, light, tools, and scouting
- provide shortcuts and alternate routes

### 3. Armory / Foundry

A dangerous equipment progression zone.

Purpose:

- better weapons
- shields
- armor upgrades
- tools
- rope
- pitons
- ammunition
- weapon repairs
- traps tied to greed and noise

### 4. Menagerie / Kennels

A monster containment area where some cages are still locked and others are broken open.

Purpose:

- roaming threats
- dangerous shortcuts
- obvious prize temptations
- monster-behavior puzzles
- risk-heavy exploration

### 5. Arcane Vault

A magical sector with spell components, ritual locks, arcane traps, strange devices, and caster resources.

Purpose:

- recover spellcasting tools
- support casters without giving them everything up front
- gate routes behind components, focuses, scrolls, or spell-slot costs
- provide high-risk crystal rooms

### 6. Prison Depths

Older, darker, less controlled sections of the dungeon.

Purpose:

- optional danger
- hidden prize rooms
- dead contestants
- strange clues
- undead or aberrant threats
- route to the Black Crystal

### 7. Escape Route

The final route out.

Purpose:

- force the last survival decisions
- tempt players with final rewards
- create a final countdown or pursuit
- separate those who leave from those who go back for more
- require all seven crystals to open the final seal

## Gear-Gated Exploration

The dungeon should contain areas the players cannot fully access at the start.

Examples:

- broken bridges requiring rope or pitons
- vertical shafts requiring climbing gear
- locked gates requiring keys or tools
- arcane seals requiring components or focuses
- holy barriers requiring a symbol or divine item
- flooded tunnels requiring rope, light, or breathing solutions
- heavy doors requiring leverage or multiple characters
- narrow passages requiring armor or gear to be removed

This makes equipment matter and turns exploration into progression.

## Trap Design

Traps should do more than deal damage.

Good traps should:

- separate players
- create noise
- attract monsters
- consume resources
- punish greed
- force hard choices
- change the room state
- create panic
- delay escape

Avoid traps that are only “roll Dexterity or take damage.” They should create situations.

Example trap types:

- screaming doors that summon patrols
- false safe rooms
- crystal traps
- gas rooms
- collapsing floors
- cursed prize items
- chain harpoons
- locked rooms with countdown hazards
- traps that target whoever carries the most crystals

## Puzzle Design

Puzzles should feel like D&D dungeon problems, not abstract riddles.

Good puzzles include:

- doors that require sacrifice
- rooms that require roleplay or interaction
- monster-behavior puzzles
- patrol-avoidance problems
- trap rooms with visible clues
- gear-based traversal challenges
- prisoners or mechanisms that create cooperation problems
- choices between escape and reward

A puzzle should not stall the entire game. It should create pressure, decision-making, or risk.

## Example Room: Grolvikk’s Last Laugh

**Grolvikk the Last Laugh** was once a human jester kept by Xhal’theris. He entertained prisoners before they were sent deeper into the dungeon. His jokes were crude, fearless, and usually aimed at whoever thought themselves too important to be mocked.

Then one day, Grolvikk told a joke that did not land.

Xhal’theris turned him to stone and left him in the dungeon as part of the game.

In this room, a statue of Grolvikk stands with one finger extended. A clue encourages the players to help him finish “the oldest joke in the world.” If they pull the finger, the doors slam shut and the room fills with a stronger version of *Stinking Cloud*.

The trap cannot be stopped mechanically. The players must finish the joke by laughing, applauding, heckling, praising Grolvikk, or otherwise playing along with the performance.

When they solve it, the gas stops, the doors unlock, and a hidden compartment opens in the statue’s base, revealing a **Green Crystal**.

## Roaming Enemy System

Roaming enemies should keep the dungeon moving.

Noise, delay, combat, failed traps, and loud spells should increase danger.

Possible roaming threats:

- jailer patrols
- escaped beasts
- animated armor
- intellect devourer scouts
- undead prisoners
- chained brutes
- aberrant hunters
- Xhal’theris’s collector drones
- a major “Warden” creature that punishes stalling

Roaming monsters should not always be fair fights. Sometimes the correct answer is to hide, run, distract, or sacrifice resources.

## Tone

The tone should be:

- lethal
- tense
- cruel
- funny in places
- practical
- fast-moving
- player-driven

The dungeon should feel like a spectacle built by a sadistic intelligence, but it should still operate like a real place with mechanisms, prisoners, monsters, tools, and routes.

The players should feel that they can survive by being careful, clever, and lucky.

They should also feel that greed can absolutely get them killed.

## Player-Facing Intro

The dungeon is run by **Xhal’theris, the Velvet Maw**, a Mind Flayer who has taken you prisoner and dropped you into his private death game.

This is a massive physical dungeon built with **Dwarven Forge terrain**, stretching roughly **5' x 7'** across the play area. It includes dozens of rooms, corridors, traps, puzzles, hazards, monsters, hidden areas, and escape routes. What you see on the table is the dungeon.

You start split into small groups in different parts of the dungeon. You have basic light armor, basic weapons, and a few scraps of spell components. That’s it. Anything better has to be found, stolen, or taken from something you kill.

There are seven prize crystals hidden throughout the dungeon, one of each color. If you die, you’re out, and whatever you were carrying stays behind.

The exit cannot open until all seven crystals are collected and placed into the exit lock.

Prize crystals come in seven tiers, and each has a matching clue phrase visible through **The Scrying Stone** that hints where another crystal can be found:

Short rests take **15 minutes of real time**. You get **three total**, and each one only lets you spend **one Hit Die**.

The goal is simple:

**Find all seven crystals. Open the exit. Get out alive.**

## Design Goal

This one-shot is not about completing a balanced adventure.

It is about putting players inside a huge dungeon and watching them make hard decisions under pressure.

The best moments should come from:

- someone risking death for a crystal
- a group deciding whether to leave someone behind
- players realizing they need gear they ignored earlier
- a room becoming dangerous because they got greedy
- someone escaping with a prize at the last possible second
- someone dying because they pushed one room too far

That is the heart of the dungeon.
